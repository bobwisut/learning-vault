A single panning noise layer looks mechanical — the same pattern looping past. Two layers at different speeds interact and produce patterns that feel alive, because the combination never exactly repeats in the same way.

<FadeIn delay={0.1}>

  The combined and final panels show what their interaction produces.
</FadeIn>

The useful mental model is:

```text
UV Layer A → Offset(t × speedA) ↘
                                  Blend → Contrast → Output
UV Layer B → Offset(t × speedB) ↗
```

## Visual Hook

<TwoLayerUVPanningDemo />

The key insight is that the combined output is more complex than either layer alone:

- Layer A pans one direction at one speed
- Layer B pans a different direction at a different speed
- Where they overlap, the blend amplifies or reveals structure
- The result evolves continuously — neither layer completes its cycle at the same time

## What Each Step Does

**UV Layer A** scrolls a coarser noise pattern in one direction. On its own it looks like a simple sliding texture.

**UV Layer B** scrolls a finer pattern at a different speed and angle. On its own it also looks simple.

**Blend** is where the complexity comes from. Two blend modes:

- **Multiply** — only areas where *both* layers are bright stay bright. Darker, more defined shapes with strong contrast.
- **Add** — areas where either layer is bright contribute to the result. Brighter overall, with flaring hotspots.

**Contrast** stretches the blended values across the full 0–1 range, making the result crisper and more usable as a mask or texture input.

## Why the Speed Ratio Matters

If both layers move at the same speed, they behave like one layer — no new complexity. The interesting behavior comes from the *difference* in speed. A 2:1 ratio means the combined pattern shifts visibly every cycle. A 7:3 ratio creates longer-period variation before the combination repeats.

Try setting both sliders to the same value and watching the combined panel go flat.

## Three Useful Presets

- **Water:** slow base with faster ripple, multiplied. Creates shifting troughs and peaks.
- **Magma:** both layers slow and coarse. The long-period interaction produces churning blobs.
- **Energy:** fast layers added. Bright hotspots flare where both layers briefly align.

## Common Mistakes

- Using the same scale for both layers. Different scales create different detail levels, which is most of where the richness comes from.
- Adding too many layers. Two well-chosen layers are usually enough; three or more often just look noisy.
- Forgetting to stretch contrast after blending. Multiply especially compresses the output range — the final contrast step recovers the full signal.

## Shader Graph Equivalent

In Unity Shader Graph, this is two **Tiling And Offset** nodes — each **Offset**
is **Time** times a different speed — feeding two **Simple Noise** (or **Sample
Texture 2D**) nodes, combined with a **Multiply** or **Add**, then a **Contrast**
node to recover the range. This page teaches the node wiring only; it does not
generate a real `.shadergraph` asset.
